Two Models of Medicine

In brief: All scientific knowledge is filtered through conceptual frameworks that dictate what we perceive, how we interpret data, and which clinical questions we ask. Medicine relies on two complementary paradigms: the pathogenic model, which focuses on the causes and mechanisms of disease, and the salutogenic model, which focuses on the processes that generate, maintain, and restore health. While modern biomedicine has built a sophisticated global infrastructure around pathogenesis, salutogenesis remains structurally fragmented. Achieving a genuinely integrated medicine requires more than appending complementary therapies to conventional care—it demands a rigorous examination of the conceptual, ontological, and epistemological foundations through which we understand health itself.

Foundations: Percepts, Concepts, and Paradigms

A common assumption holds that scientific knowledge advances simply by accumulating sensory data, a view often termed naive realism. Yet sensory data alone is insufficient. Raw perceptual information (percepts) becomes intelligible only through organizing structures (concepts). As Thomas Kuhn demonstrated, scientific communities never gather facts in a vacuum; observation is inherently mediated by paradigms that dictate which questions are valid, which evidence is privileged, and what counts as real.1

A paradigm functions less as a complete view of reality than as a selective spotlight, bringing certain phenomena into sharp focus while leaving others in the dark. The danger is not that frameworks exist—some lens is unavoidable—but that we so easily mistake the illuminated area for the whole room, dismissing whatever falls outside as irrelevant, anomalous, or nonexistent.

In medicine, this hidden framework dictates not only how health and disease are understood, but which clinical observations matter and which interventions are deemed legitimate. Ultimately, it defines what medicine is equipped to see.

Two primary models have shaped our clinical vision. The pathogenic model, embodied by modern biomedicine, focuses on identifying and eliminating disease. By contrast, the salutogenic model focuses on what generates health and restores systemic coherence—a framework that forms the core philosophy behind many traditional medical systems. Training a clinician to think and practice within a salutogenic framework requires an entirely different cognitive orientation than training them in pathogenesis. Yet because salutogenesis lacks a comprehensive, modern pedagogical framework, practitioners steeped in pathogenic training have no unified foundation to learn from, leaving them to apply salutogenic therapies through a fundamentally pathogenic lens.

A central thesis of the Salutogenic Medicine Project is that biomedicine's exclusive focus on pathology has marginalized salutogenic frameworks, particularly where conventional care reaches its explanatory limits. Rather than accepting or dismissing salutogenic ideas on ideology alone, the Project subjects these paradigms to rigorous empirical scrutiny. Crucially, it does so without reducing health generation to existing biomedical mechanisms, by honoring the distinct ontology and epistemology of living systems while building the conceptual, educational, clinical, and research infrastructure needed to train practitioners, advance scientific discovery, and expand biomedicine into a truly comprehensive model of care.

Pathogenesis: The Study of Disease

To understand what salutogenesis offers, we must first examine the paradigm that currently defines modern biomedical care. Pathogenesis refers broadly to the mechanisms through which disease arises and develops. Its organizing questions focus on identifying causal factors, characterizing pathological processes, and developing interventions to prevent, modify, or reverse them.

This focus developed through key scientific shifts that progressively established disease as something that could be localized, measured, and targeted. This shift began in the nineteenth century with Rudolf Virchow's localization of pathology to specific cellular changes,2 followed by Pasteur and Koch's demonstration of microbial causes for infectious disease.3 Subsequent advances in genetics, biochemistry, and molecular biology further refined our ability to isolate increasingly precise disease mechanisms.

Today's healthcare architecture directly reflects this history. Medical training, diagnostic criteria, laboratory testing, imaging, pharmacology, surgery, and clinical trials are all primarily organized around identifying pathology and disrupting its progression.

The achievements of this approach are monumental. Vaccination, antimicrobial therapy, surgical techniques, trauma care, and targeted molecular treatments have dramatically reduced morbidity and mortality worldwide. Pathogenesis is exceptionally powerful when a discrete pathological process can be isolated and targeted with high specificity. Yet this same strength defines its characteristic limits.

The Limits of the Pathogenic Model

Despite its extraordinary power to explain and interrupt disease, the pathogenic model does not constitute a complete theory of health. It excels at identifying how disease arises and can be targeted, but it struggles to explain why health is maintained under continual perturbation, or why individuals exposed to identical stressors experience vastly different outcomes. Optimized for discrete, acute events, the model reaches its boundaries when applied to phenomena that lack a single, isolatable cause.

At a fundamental level, the pathogenic model seeks material causes—reducing every health phenomenon to physical, mechanistic interactions. Yet in complex chronic illness, functional disorders, and mental distress, causality is rarely single or purely mechanical. Rather, health and illness emerge from a dynamic interplay across multiple dimensions of the human being: physical, physiological, psychological, relational, and spiritual. Forcing these multidimensional dynamics into a strictly reductionist frame fails to capture how living systems actually operate.

This epistemological bottleneck actively shapes the structure of care. Because the model isolates individual components to find specific physical mechanisms, it has organized modern healthcare into hyper-specialized, disconnected silos. While this engineering approach succeeds in acute crises, it falters when care is fragmented across departments that each manage a single organ while missing the human being as a whole. The result is an administrative assembly line increasingly collapsing under its own structural weight.

On a human level, this reductionist framing strips the clinical encounter of personal meaning. By reducing the living person to a vessel for physical pathology, the model often leaves patients feeling objectified and disempowered. For clinicians, working within a transactional system that ignores both the body's intrinsic capacity for self-organization and the patient's inner life contributes directly to moral injury, burnout, and a loss of professional purpose.

When a paradigm reaches these limits, clinical care cannot remain restricted to identifying and eliminating a single material mechanism. The standard pathogenic question—what is wrong, and how do we fix it?—remains necessary, but insufficient. A complete clinical inquiry must also ask what adaptive capacities remain intact, what physiological processes are sustaining function despite disease, and what multidimensional factors are impairing those processes. Crucially, it must ask how to strengthen the individual's capacity for self-organization, recovery, and resilience, while honoring what gives their life purpose, coherence, and meaning. These questions do not displace pathogenesis; they expand the field of vision to restore wholeness to clinical care.

Salutogenesis: The Study of Health

The term salutogenesis was introduced by medical sociologist Aaron Antonovsky in the 1970s.4 Investigating why some individuals maintain health despite severe stress and adversity, Antonovsky made a crucial conceptual breakthrough: he rejected the traditional binary that treats health and disease as mutually exclusive states. Instead, he conceptualized health as a dynamic ease/dis-ease continuum. The core question was no longer what categorized someone as "sick," but what factors actively move a person toward the health end of the spectrum. Antonovsky highlighted the role of psychosocial resilience and what he termed the sense of coherence—a global orientation where one's world is experienced as comprehensible, manageable, and meaningful.

The instinct behind salutogenesis, however, is far older than the word. Traditional medical systems—such as Traditional Chinese Medicine, Ayurveda, and Western Vitalist traditions—were organized around supporting the body's self-regulatory capacity centuries before biomedicine focused on pathogenesis. Modern science has since built a compelling, evidence-based foundation for this same intrinsic capacity, mapped through homeostasis, allostasis, psychoneuroimmunology, network physiology, and bioelectric field regulation.

While Antonovsky developed his model primarily within the psychosocial realm, the salutogenic question applies across the entire human being. If health can be generated through psychosocial processes, analogous, health-generating dynamics must operate at every level of our biology and experience. A whole-person salutogenic model extends Antonovsky's vision into four distinct, interconnected dimensions of the individual:

Physical dimension: The body's material substance and spatial structure—tissues, organs, and molecular composition—the realm modern biomedicine examines most directly. This layer is continually acted upon by its material environment, from physical trauma to ecological inputs and toxic burdens.

Physiological dimension: The body's dynamic, self-organizing activity and biological rhythms—including neuroendocrine regulation, autonomic balance, tissue repair, and immune response. This rhythmic layer is profoundly influenced by natural cycles, environmental entrainment, and the harmonizing effects of the living world.

Psychological dimension: Emotional regulation, coping strategies, stress appraisal, and relational dynamics. This layer shapes—and is shaped by—our social environment, interpersonal bonds, and immediate cultural context.

Noetic (Mental-Spiritual) dimension: Belief systems, alignment with values, self-transcendence, and a felt sense of purpose—the domain toward which Antonovsky's "meaningfulness" component gestures. This sphere reflects our relationship to truth, orientation, and ultimate meaning.

Each dimension operates according to its own distinct principles. Physiological regulation, for example, follows dynamics like allostasis and biological rhythmicity, which cannot be reduced to simple physical mechanics, nor directly mapped onto psychological or noetic registers without translation. The chapters that follow examine how these four dimensions interact, how they maintain systemic coherence, and how clinicians can actively support health across every level of the human being.

The Need for a Modern Salutogenic Framework

The movement toward integrative and whole-person medicine arises, in part, as a direct response to the limits of a purely disease-centered model. Yet integration today often means adding therapeutic modalities rather than synthesizing conceptual frameworks. In practice, the conventional biomedical model remains the core organizing structure, while nutritional, botanical, behavioral, traditional, or mind-body therapies are simply appended to standard care. While clinically valuable, assembling a menu of diverse modalities does not constitute genuine theoretical integration.

Historical medical traditions offer crucial context for why this synthesis remains elusive. Systems such as Traditional Chinese Medicine, Ayurveda, and Western Vitalist traditions developed sophisticated frameworks for maintaining balance, supporting vitality, and preserving health. These systems should neither be romanticized nor dismissed. Although their explanatory models reflect distinct historical and cultural contexts, the cross-cultural recurrence of these health-generating principles points to a persistent reality: these traditions repeatedly observed dynamics of self-regulation and adaptation that contemporary science is only now beginning to map through modern methods.

The task, therefore, is neither to reduce every traditional concept to a known biomedical mechanism nor to discard biomedicine in favor of traditional philosophy. Rather, it is to determine whether a broader conceptual framework can accommodate valid observations from both traditional systems and modern science while maintaining rigorous empirical standards.

Accredited naturopathic medicine represents one of the most visible attempts to build such an integrated model, and its structural challenges are instructive. While nominally guided by a vitalist, salutogenic philosophy, naturopathic education and clinical support tools rely heavily on biomedical science. Salutogenic modalities—such as hydrotherapy, botanical medicine, and homeopathy—are frequently taught alongside this biomedical core rather than integrated with it into a unified framework. Furthermore, each modality often retains its own isolated theory, with little systematic connection to the others or to modern physiological mechanisms. Practices categorized as "energy medicine" sit at an even further remove, pursuing salutogenic goals while operating largely outside contemporary scientific standards.

That many naturopathic clinicians seek extensive post-graduate training in classical Chinese medicine or other traditional systems suggests that current integrative education does not yet provide a fully unified salutogenic philosophy. This is not a failure of naturopathic medicine, but a symptom of a much larger structural gap: no cross-traditional, scientifically grounded foundation comparable to biomedicine currently exists for salutogenic practice.

To fill this gap, a modern salutogenic framework must account for both the mechanisms of disease and the systemic processes through which health emerges, adapts, and persists. Building that foundational architecture is the ultimate objective of the Salutogenic Medicine Project.

Shallow and Deep Salutogenesis

Developing a truly unified framework requires examining assumptions far deeper than terminology. The fundamental differences between medical paradigms concern not only the concepts they use, but their implicit assumptions about what exists (ontology) and how that reality can be known (epistemology).

Ontology addresses the nature of reality: what constitutes a disease, what defines health, what an organism is, and how living systems relate to their environment.

Epistemology addresses the nature and limits of knowledge: what counts as evidence, how phenomena are observed and measured, and which methods can produce reliable truth.

These distinctions are critical because a health model can acknowledge multiple dimensions of a human being—physical, physiological, psychological, and noetic—while remaining strictly bound to a materialist ontology. On this view, the physical dimension is primary, while physiological regulation, emotion, and meaning are treated as emergent epiphenomena built upward from a physical substrate. In principle, such models assume these higher dimensions can be described by physical metrics alone.

Multiplying levels in this way is an advance over crude reductionism because it broadens clinical attention. But it does not alter what is assumed to be ultimately real. This is shallow salutogenesis: an approach that expands the clinical picture across multiple layers while remaining, ontologically and epistemologically, inside the materialist boundaries of modern biomedicine.

Deep salutogenesis poses a far more radical question: whether these non-physical dimensions might be ontologically real in their own right. Rather than treating physiological rhythms, psychological states, and noetic meaning as convenient descriptive categories emergent from physical matter, deep salutogenesis asks if each dimension functions as an autonomous "space"—much like a mathematical space—operating under unique laws not reducible to physical-material mechanics.

In this view, just as a higher-dimensional mathematical space contains distinct topological objects and field properties governed by its own internal axioms, each dimension of the human organism contains its own "objects" and dynamics—from self-organizing physiological field states and biological rhythms, to psychological structures and noetic meaning. The physical body is not the sole source of these phenomena, but the material locus where these distinct spaces intersect and express themselves.

If so, a profound epistemological question follows: what mode of knowing could actually perceive such domains? Ordinary sensory observation and instrumented measurement were designed specifically to measure physical mechanisms and spatial residue. Navigating a non-material space requires an extension of our cognitive capacities—a mode of perception cultivated to apprehend pattern, process, and structural laws directly within those domains, rather than merely measuring their downstream physical footprints.

The difference between shallow and deep salutogenesis is not merely a matter of degree, or of one approach being "more holistic" than another. It is a difference in kind. It turns on whether the physicalist assumptions of modern biomedicine are simply stretched across broader categories, or whether the human being is recognized as a multidimensional reality composed of distinct, ontologically real spaces.

Towards an Integrative Medicine

Pathogenesis and salutogenesis are complementary orientations toward the same living reality, not competing medical systems. Pathogenesis is indispensable when a specific pathology calls for targeted intervention; salutogenesis becomes essential for prevention, chronic illness, recovery, and the restoration of self-organizing capacities that resist reduction to a single material cause.

The true distinction lies in how we conceptualize the clinical problem, not in artificial divides between conventional and alternative medicine or biomedical and traditional therapies. The same patient, the same physiological system, and the same therapeutic encounter may call for both modes of reasoning at once.

Yet a profound structural asymmetry remains. The pathogenic model possesses a fully fleshed-out institutional architecture: standardized medical curricula, authoritative textbooks, established research paradigms, diagnostic coding, and robust clinical decision-support tools. Salutogenesis has no equivalent infrastructure. While its principles are reflected in various traditional systems and modern research niches, it lacks a unified educational core, a shared clinical toolkit, and an overarching research paradigm.

Closing this gap is a central mission of the Salutogenic Medicine Project. The goal is not to construct a competing dogma to displace biomedicine, but to build the rigorous conceptual, educational, and clinical foundation that salutogenic practice currently lacks—yielding a more comprehensive medicine capable of explaining both how health is lost and how it is generated, maintained, and restored.

Operationalizing that unified framework means determining which dimension of the human being a given intervention primarily engages:

  • Physical: Acting on material substance and spatial structure—repairing tissue, correcting a biochemical deficiency, or removing a lesion—the domain where pathogenic targeting operates most directly.

  • Physiological: Supporting the body's native, self-regulatory rhythms and adaptive processes—such as neuroendocrine balance, immune regulation, or tissue repair—rather than suppressing or overriding them. This is where pathogenic and salutogenic reasoning most directly meet.

  • Psychological: Influencing emotional regulation, stress appraisal, coping strategies, and relational health—the domain captured by Antonovsky's sense of coherence.

  • Noetic (Mental-Spiritual): Engaging belief systems, core values, orientation, and purpose—the dimension of deep meaning that lies beyond physical mechanism.

An intervention may act across several of these dimensions simultaneously, and evaluating its efficacy requires methods appropriate to the specific levels it engages.

The chapters that follow develop this architecture systematically. The next chapter examines salutogenesis in depth, beginning with Antonovsky's original model and tracing its evolution through modern homeostasis, allostasis, network physiology, and bioelectric field regulation. From there, it takes up the full distinction between shallow and deep salutogenesis: exploring what a multi-layered, non-materialist ontology actually claims, what extended epistemology can support it, and why this distinction marks a fundamental shift in how we understand human health.
