Summary

  • All scientific knowledge is filtered through conceptual frameworks that shape what we perceive, how we interpret data, and which clinical questions we even think to ask.
  • The pathogenic model asks what causes disease; the salutogenic model asks what generates and sustains health.
  • Biomedicine has built a sophisticated infrastructure around the first; the second remains structurally fragmented.
  • Genuine integration requires examining both models' conceptual, ontological, and epistemological foundations—not just appending complementary therapies to conventional care.

Foundations: Percepts, Concepts, and Paradigms

A common assumption holds that scientific knowledge advances simply by accumulating sensory data, a view often termed naive realism. Yet sensory data alone is insufficient. Raw perceptual information (percepts) becomes intelligible only through organizing structures (concepts). As the philosopher of science Thomas Kuhn demonstrated, scientific communities never gather facts in a vacuum.1 Instead, observation is inherently mediated by paradigms that dictate which questions are valid, which evidence is privileged, and what counts as real.

Crucially, conceptual models are not merely passive observational tools. They function as both epistemological frameworks, shaping how we know and gather evidence, and ontological commitments, defining what we believe actually exists. They are a priori lenses rather than empirical facts to be proven "true" or "false" in an absolute sense. A model is judged not by whether it mirrors ultimate reality, but by its utility: the depth of insight it yields, the clinical problems it solves, and the therapeutic possibilities it opens. When we forget this distinction, we commit a category error, mistaking our epistemological map for the ontological territory itself.

In medicine, these unconscious paradigms dictate not only how health and disease are understood, but which clinical observations matter and which interventions are deemed legitimate. These hidden assumptions filter every diagnostic test, dictate treatment guidelines, and determine whether a patient's lived experience is treated as vital clinical data or discarded as irrelevant. Ultimately, an unexamined framework defines what medicine is equipped to see, and by extension, what it remains blind to in daily clinical practice.

Throughout history, two primary conceptual models have shaped our clinical vision. Deeply embedded in the institutional architecture of modern biomedicine, the pathogenic model focuses on identifying, localizing, and eliminating disease. In contrast, the salutogenic model, the historic organizing principle of many traditional medical systems, explores the processes that generate health, cultivate resilience, and restore systemic coherence.

Training a clinician to think and practice within a salutogenic framework requires an entirely different cognitive orientation than training them in pathogenesis. Yet because salutogenesis lacks a comprehensive, modern pedagogical framework, practitioners steeped in pathogenic training have no unified foundation from which to learn, often leaving them to apply salutogenic or integrative therapies through a fundamentally pathogenic lens.

Modern biomedicine's near-exclusive focus on pathology has historically marginalized salutogenic frameworks, particularly where conventional care reaches its explanatory limits. Rather than accepting or dismissing salutogenic ideas on ideological grounds, we must construct the conceptual, educational, clinical, and research infrastructure necessary to operationalize them with scientific rigor. Crucially, this effort must not collapse salutogenesis into existing reductionist mechanisms. Instead, it must honor the distinct ontology and epistemology of health generation, expanding medicine toward a far more comprehensive vision of human life.

Pathogenesis: The Study of Disease

To understand what salutogenesis offers, we must first examine the paradigm that currently defines modern medical care. Pathogenesis refers broadly to the mechanisms through which disease arises and develops. Its organizing questions focus on identifying material causal factors, characterizing pathological mechanisms, and developing interventions to prevent, modify, or reverse them.

The pathogenic framework developed through key scientific shifts that progressively established disease as something that could be localized, measured, and targeted. This shift began in the nineteenth century with Rudolf Virchow’s localization of pathology away from bodily humors to specific cellular changes, followed by Pasteur and Koch’s demonstration of microbial, rather than supernatural, causes for infectious disease.2,3 Subsequent advances in genetics, biochemistry, and molecular biology further refined our ability to isolate increasingly precise disease mechanisms.

Today’s healthcare architecture directly reflects this history. Medical training, diagnostic criteria, laboratory testing, imaging, pharmacology, surgery, clinical trials, research funding, evidence-based medicine, and even insurance reimbursement are all primarily organized around identifying pathology and disrupting its progression.

The achievements of this approach are monumental. Vaccination, antimicrobial therapy, surgical techniques, trauma care, and targeted molecular treatments have dramatically reduced morbidity and mortality worldwide. Pathogenesis is exceptionally powerful when a discrete pathological process can be isolated and targeted with high specificity.

The Limits of the Pathogenic Model

Despite its extraordinary power to explain and interrupt disease, the pathogenic model does not constitute a complete theory of health. It excels at identifying how disease arises and can be targeted, but it struggles to explain why health is maintained under continual perturbation, or why individuals exposed to identical stressors experience vastly different outcomes. Optimized for discrete, acute events, the model reaches its boundaries when applied to phenomena that lack a single, isolatable cause.

Beneath this limitation lies a fundamental philosophical constraint: the model restricts its inquiry almost exclusively to material causes. Tracing back to Aristotle’s classic taxonomy of causation—material, efficient, formal, and final 4—modern biomedicine excels at isolating tangible triggers of pathology, such as pathogens, genetic mutations, or physical lesions.

Crucially, this same reductionist habit often persists within complementary and integrative medicine. The popular rally cry to “treat the cause” (tolle causam) is frequently celebrated as a departure from conventional medicine. Yet, in practice, it subtlely retains the very same pathogenic mindset, assuming there is a single, material root cause (a hidden microbe, heavy metal, allergen, or nutrient deficiency) waiting to be pinpointed and excised.

A truly salutogenic framework engages causation far more broadly. While acknowledging material factors, it expands its focus to include:

  • Efficient causes: The dynamic, self-regulatory drivers and metabolic vectors of physiology.

  • Formal causes: The organizing informational matrices and spatial configurations of the terrain—encompassing both biophysical field topologies (such as bioelectric patterns) and psychological architectures (such as emotional attractor states, core belief schemas, and psycho-somatic organization).

  • Final causes: Purpose, teleology, existential orientation, and the noetic drive toward wholeness and coherence.

Forcing these multidimensional dynamics into a strictly material frame does more than distort our understanding of living systems—it fundamentally dictates how healthcare is delivered. Because the pathogenic model isolates individual components to pinpoint specific physical mechanisms, it has organized modern medicine into hyper-specialized, disconnected silos. While this engineering approach excels in acute crises, it falters when chronic care is fragmented across departments that each manage a single organ while ignoring the human being as a whole. The result is an administrative assembly line increasingly collapsing under its own structural weight.

On a human level, this reductionist framing strips the clinical encounter of personal meaning. By reducing the living person to a vessel for physical pathology, the model often leaves patients feeling objectified and disempowered. For clinicians, working within a transactional system that ignores both the body’s intrinsic capacity for self-organization and the patient’s inner life contributes directly to moral injury, burnout, and a loss of professional purpose.

When a paradigm reaches these limits, clinical care cannot remain restricted to identifying and eliminating a single material mechanism. The standard pathogenic question—what is the cause, and how do we fix it?—remains necessary, but insufficient. A complete clinical inquiry must also ask what adaptive capacities remain intact, what physiological processes are sustaining function despite disease, and what multidimensional factors are impairing those processes. Crucially, it must ask how to strengthen the individual’s capacity for self-organization, recovery, and resilience, while honoring what gives their life purpose, coherence, and meaning. These questions do not displace pathogenesis; they expand the field of vision to restore wholeness to clinical care.

Salutogenesis: The Study of Health

The term salutogenesis was introduced by medical sociologist Aaron Antonovsky in the 1970s.5 Investigating why some individuals maintain health despite severe stress and adversity, Antonovsky made a crucial conceptual breakthrough: he rejected the traditional binary that treats health and disease as mutually exclusive states. Instead, he conceptualized health as a dynamic ease/dis-ease continuum. The core question was no longer what categorized someone as “sick,” but what factors actively move a person toward the health end of the spectrum. Antonovsky highlighted the role of psychosocial resilience and what he termed the sense of coherence, a global orientation where one’s world is experienced as comprehensible, manageable, and meaningful.

The instinct behind salutogenesis, however, is far older than the word. Traditional medical systems—such as Traditional Chinese Medicine, Ayurveda, and Western Vitalist traditions—were organized around supporting the body’s self-regulatory capacity centuries before biomedicine focused on pathogenesis. Modern science has since built a compelling, evidence-based foundation for this same intrinsic capacity, grounding the generic is medicatrix naturae principle of Hippocrates with the sciences of homeostasis, allostasis, psychoneuroimmunology, network physiology, and bioelectric field regulation.

While Antonovsky developed his model primarily within the psychosocial realm, the salutogenic question applies across the entire human being. If health can be generated through psychosocial processes, analogous, health-generating dynamics must operate at every level of our biology and experience.

A whole-person salutogenic model extends Antonovsky’s vision into four distinct, interconnected dimensions of the individual:

  • Physical dimension: The body’s material substance and spatial structure—tissues, organs, and molecular composition—the realm modern biomedicine examines most directly. This layer is continually acted upon by its material environment, from physical trauma to ecological inputs and toxic burdens.

  • Physiological dimension: The body’s dynamic, self-organizing activity and biological rhythms, including neuroendocrine regulation, autonomic balance, tissue repair, and immune response. This rhythmic layer is profoundly influenced by natural cycles, environmental entrainment, and the harmonizing effects of the living world.

  • Psychological dimension: Emotional regulation, coping strategies, stress appraisal, and relational dynamics. This layer shapes, and is shaped by, our social environment, interpersonal bonds, and immediate cultural context.

  • Noetic (Mental-Spiritual) dimension: Belief systems, alignment with values, self-transcendence, and a felt sense of purpose—the domain toward which Antonovsky’s “meaningfulness” component gestures. This sphere reflects our relationship to truth, orientation, and ultimate meaning.

Each dimension operates according to its own distinct principles. Physiological regulation, for example, follows dynamics like allostasis and biological rhythmicity, which cannot be reduced to simple physical mechanics, nor directly mapped onto psychological or noetic registers without translation.

The Need for a Modern Salutogenic Framework

The movement toward integrative and whole-person medicine arises, in part, as a direct response to the limits of a purely disease-centered model. Yet integration today often means adding therapeutic modalities rather than synthesizing conceptual frameworks. In practice, the conventional biomedical model remains the core organizing structure, while nutritional, botanical, behavioral, traditional, or mind-body therapies are simply appended to standard care. While clinically valuable, assembling a menu of diverse modalities does not constitute genuine theoretical integration.

Historical medical traditions offer crucial context for why this synthesis remains elusive. Systems such as Traditional Chinese Medicine, Ayurveda, and Western Vitalist traditions developed sophisticated frameworks for maintaining balance, supporting vitality, and preserving health. These systems shouldn’t be romanticized, but neither should they be dismissed. Although their explanatory models reflect distinct historical and cultural contexts, the cross-cultural recurrence of these health-generating principles points to a persistent reality: these traditions repeatedly observed dynamics of self-regulation and adaptation that contemporary science is only now beginning to map through modern methods.

The task, therefore, is neither to reduce every traditional concept to a known biomedical mechanism nor to discard biomedicine in favor of traditional philosophy. Rather, it is to determine whether a broader conceptual framework can accommodate valid observations from both traditional systems and modern science while maintaining rigorous empirical standards.

Accredited naturopathic medicine represents one of the most visible attempts to build such an integrated model, and its structural challenges are instructive. While nominally guided by a vitalist, salutogenic philosophy, naturopathic education and clinical support tools rely heavily on biomedical science. Salutogenic modalities—such as hydrotherapy, botanical medicine, and homeopathy—are frequently taught alongside this biomedical core rather than integrated with it into a unified framework. Furthermore, each modality often retains its own isolated theory, with little systematic connection to the others or to modern physiological mechanisms. Practices categorized as “energy medicine” sit at an even further remove, pursuing salutogenic goals while operating largely outside contemporary scientific standards.

That many naturopathic clinicians seek extensive post-graduate training in classical Chinese medicine or other traditional systems suggests that current integrative education does not yet provide a fully unified salutogenic philosophy. This is not a failure of naturopathic medicine, but a symptom of a much larger structural gap: no cross-traditional, scientifically grounded foundation comparable to biomedicine currently exists for salutogenic practice.

To fill this gap, a modern salutogenic framework must account for both the mechanisms of disease and the systemic processes through which health emerges, adapts, and persists. Building that foundational architecture is an urgent, necessary task for the future of medicine.

Shallow and Deep Salutogenesis

Developing a truly unified medical framework requires examining assumptions far deeper than clinical terminology. A health model can readily acknowledge multiple human dimensions—physical, physiological, psychological, and noetic—while remaining strictly bound to a physicalist framework.

In recent decades, systems biology, network physiology, and the various “-omics” disciplines have taken a crucial step beyond simple reductionism. By focusing on multi-scale networks and dynamic biological feedback, these fields recognize emergent properties that cannot be predicted by analyzing isolated molecular parts alone.

Yet, despite this sophisticated complexity, systems biology remains anchored in the foundational premises of modern biomedicine. It operates from a strictly bottom-up, physicalist framework: assuming that physiological regulation, emotional states, and noetic meaning are ultimately secondary epiphenomena generated by an underlying physical substrate, describable in principle by material metrics alone.

Expanding clinical attention across network layers is a vital advancement over crude reductionism, but it does not alter what is assumed to be fundamentally real. This is shallow salutogenesis: an approach that broadens the clinical picture across multiple biological and psychosocial levels while remaining entirely inside the physicalist boundaries of modern biomedicine.

Deep salutogenesis poses a far more radical question: whether these non-physical dimensions might be real in their own right, rather than merely emergent properties of physical matter. Rather than treating physiological rhythms, psychological architectures, and noetic meaning as convenient descriptive categories built upward from molecules, deep salutogenesis asks if each dimension functions as an autonomous, higher-order domain, operating under native laws that exert top-down causation on the physical layers below them. This represents a fundamental alignment with traditional medical systems, which historically viewed physical tissue not as the sole generator of life, but as the downstream recipient of formal, organizing principles.

From this perspective, the human organism can be understood as a multi-dimensional topology. Just as higher-dimensional mathematical spaces house distinct geometric objects governed by internal axioms, each dimension of human existence contains its own native structures and dynamics—spanning self-organizing physiological field states, biological rhythms, psychological configurations, and noetic meaning. The physical body is not the primary origin of these dynamics, but rather the material locus where these distinct spaces intersect, interact, and express themselves.

Developmental biologist Michael Levin has already operationalized this shift by showing how organisms navigate an "anatomical morphospace" to reach specific target geometries during regeneration and development.6 This concept resonates strongly with Rupert Sheldrake’s non-local morphogenetic fields.7 While such fields display measurable biophysical correlates—such as the bioelectric pattern memories that instruct tissue form—they are fundamentally informational, rather than physical, mechanical, or even energetic. Extending this logic suggests the need to formally distinguish between a physio-space, emotional-space, and noetic-space, each defined by its own native "objects," internal rules, and relational dynamics that continuously shape physical expression from the top down.

This insight raises a profound clinical question: what mode of knowing can actually perceive and engage such domains? Standard sensory observation and laboratory instrumentation were engineered specifically to capture physical mechanisms and spatial residue. Navigating non-material spaces requires extending our cognitive and diagnostic capacities—cultivating modes of perception equipped to apprehend pattern, process, and structural laws directly within those native domains, rather than merely measuring their downstream physical footprints.

The difference between shallow and deep salutogenesis is not a matter of degree, nor is it simply about one approach being “more holistic” than another. It is a difference in kind. It turns on whether physicalist assumptions are merely stretched across broader computational networks, or whether the human being is recognized as a truly multi-dimensional reality governed by interacting, ontologically real spaces.

Towards an Integrative Medicine

Pathogenesis and salutogenesis are complementary orientations toward the same living reality, not competing medical systems. Pathogenesis is indispensable when a specific pathology calls for targeted intervention. Salutogenesis becomes essential for prevention, chronic illness, recovery, and the restoration of self-organizing capacities that resist reduction to a single material cause.

The true distinction lies in how we conceptualize the clinical problem, not in artificial divides between conventional and alternative medicine or biomedical and traditional therapies. The same patient, the same physiological system, and the same therapeutic encounter may call for both modes of reasoning at once.

Yet a profound structural asymmetry remains. The pathogenic model possesses a fully fleshed-out institutional architecture: standardized medical curricula, authoritative textbooks, established research paradigms, diagnostic coding, and robust clinical decision-support tools. Salutogenesis has no equivalent infrastructure. While its principles are reflected in various traditional systems and modern research niches, it lacks a unified educational core, a shared clinical toolkit, and an overarching research paradigm.

Closing this gap is a central task for the next generation of medical thought. The goal is not to construct a competing dogma to displace biomedicine, but to build the rigorous conceptual, educational, and clinical foundation that salutogenic practice currently lacks—yielding a more comprehensive medicine capable of explaining both how health is lost and how it is generated, maintained, and restored.

Operationalizing that unified framework means determining which dimension of the human being a given intervention primarily engages:

  • Physical: Acting on material substance and spatial structure—repairing tissue, correcting a biochemical deficiency, or removing a lesion—the domain where pathogenic targeting operates most directly.

  • Physiological: Supporting the body’s native, self-regulatory rhythms and adaptive processes—such as neuroendocrine balance, immune regulation, or tissue repair—rather than suppressing or overriding them. This is where pathogenic and salutogenic reasoning most directly meet.

  • Psychological: Influencing emotional regulation, stress appraisal, coping strategies, and relational health—the domain captured by Antonovsky’s sense of coherence.

  • Noetic (Mental-Spiritual): Engaging belief systems, core values, orientation, and purpose—the dimension that taps into creativity, inner guidance, and higher meaning.

An intervention may act across several of these dimensions simultaneously, and evaluating its efficacy requires methods appropriate to the specific levels it engages.

Rebuilding medicine around this multi-dimensional reality is the defining challenge of modern healthcare. When we align our diagnostic frameworks with the full spectrum of human organization—from physical tissue to noetic purpose—we lay the groundwork for a clinical model that is both scientifically rigorous and genuinely holistic. The future of medicine will not be defined merely by how effectively we dismantle disease, but by how skillfully we cultivate the conditions for enduring health.
