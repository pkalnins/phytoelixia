In brief: Knowledge is not derived from sensory observation alone but is mediated by conceptual frameworks that determine what we perceive, how we interpret it, and which questions we regard as meaningful. Medicine can be understood as operating through two complementary models: the pathogenic model (pathogenesis), concerned with the causes and mechanisms of disease, and the salutogenic model (salutogenesis), concerned with the processes that generate, maintain, and restore health. Modern biomedicine has developed an extraordinarily sophisticated institutional and scientific infrastructure around pathogenesis, while salutogenesis remains comparatively fragmented. The development of a genuinely integrated medicine therefore requires more than the juxtaposition of biomedical and complementary therapies. It requires a systematic examination of the conceptual, ontological, and epistemological foundations through which health and disease are understood.

Foundations: Percepts, Concepts, and Paradigms

It is a common assumption that knowledge of the natural world—including knowledge of the human body, health, and disease—is acquired primarily through the accumulation and organization of sensory observations. On this view, the senses provide access to an external reality, while scientific instruments such as microscopes, imaging technologies, and molecular assays extend the range and precision of observation. Scientific progress can then be understood, at least in part, as the progressive refinement of our observations and the theories constructed from them.

This position, often characterized philosophically as naive realism, assumes that perception provides relatively direct access to an objective reality that exists independently of the observer. Although contemporary science depends fundamentally upon empirical observation, neither philosophy of science nor cognitive science supports such a straightforward account of the relationship between perception and knowledge.

Sensory information is necessary for empirical knowledge, but it is not sufficient. Perception is mediated by preexisting conceptual structures that organize sensory information into meaningful patterns. The distinction between percepts and concepts is therefore important: perceptual information provides the raw material of experience, while concepts provide the structures through which that information becomes intelligible.

In scientific disciplines, these conceptual structures operate at multiple levels. At the broadest level are the paradigms that determine which questions are considered legitimate, which observations are treated as significant, which forms of evidence are privileged, and which explanations are regarded as scientifically meaningful. Thomas Kuhn's influential analysis of scientific paradigms demonstrated that scientific communities do not simply accumulate facts in a theoretically neutral manner.1 Rather, observation and interpretation occur within conceptual frameworks that shape the problems scientists recognize and the solutions they regard as possible.

A paradigm thus functions less as a complete representation of reality than as a selective field of illumination. It makes certain phenomena highly visible while leaving others comparatively obscure. What lies outside its conceptual boundaries may not be nonexistent; it may simply fail to register as a meaningful object of inquiry.

The difficulty is not that paradigms exist. Some conceptual framework is unavoidable. The difficulty arises when the phenomena illuminated by a particular paradigm are implicitly treated as constituting the entirety of reality, while phenomena that fall outside its explanatory field are regarded as irrelevant, anomalous, or nonexistent.

This process is often largely unconscious. The conceptual assumptions that organize professional practice are typically acquired through education and reinforced by institutional structures, disciplinary norms, and prevailing cultural expectations. They become sufficiently familiar that they are experienced not as a particular way of interpreting reality, but as reality itself.

This observation has particular relevance to medicine. The conceptual framework through which clinicians are trained influences not only how disease is understood, but also how health is conceptualized, which clinical observations are considered meaningful, and which forms of intervention are regarded as legitimate. It determines, in part, what medicine is equipped to see.

A fundamental thesis of the Salutogenic Medicine Project is that modern medicine has developed primarily within a particular conceptual paradigm, while other potentially informative ways of understanding health and disease have remained largely outside its institutional and scientific framework. These alternative paradigms may offer novel insights precisely in domains where the prevailing paradigm encounters explanatory or therapeutic limitations. Their marginalization, however, does not by itself establish either their validity or their invalidity; rather, it reflects, in part, differences in the underlying assumptions about what health and disease are and how they can be known. The aim of the Salutogenic Medicine Project is therefore to examine these alternative paradigms with the same intellectual rigor applied to the prevailing paradigm, to identify which of their claims can withstand critical and empirical scrutiny, and to determine whether their valid insights can be integrated into a more comprehensive account of medicine.

Two Organizing Models of Medicine

Throughout history, two primary paradigms have shaped how we understand health and illness, each taking institutional and clinical form as a distinct model of medicine. The one most familiar is the pathogenic model, which focuses on disease and asks how to identify, explain, and eliminate it. Its primary institutional expression is modern biomedicine: the clinical and scientific apparatus organized around identifying and treating disease. The salutogenic model has no comparably unified institutional home, a gap taken up directly in later sections of this chapter.

A different model, the salutogenic model, starts with a different focus: not what causes disease, but what generates and sustains health, and how that capacity can be strengthened directly so disease has less room to take root. On this view, health is not simply the absence of disease, and disease is better understood as a loss of the body's own coherence than as an invading enemy to be defeated.

These two models are complementary rather than mutually exclusive. They may be directed toward the same patient, the same physiological system, or even the same clinical encounter. Nevertheless, they organize clinical attention differently and may lead to different forms of inquiry — a substantive difference, not merely a terminological one.

A pathogenic orientation asks, in effect: What has gone wrong? A salutogenic orientation asks: What is generating health, what is impairing that process, and what might strengthen it? The distinction becomes particularly important when the causes of illness are complex, distributed across multiple levels of organization, or not reducible to a single identifiable pathological mechanism.

Pathogenesis: The Study of Disease

Pathogenesis refers broadly to the mechanisms through which disease arises and develops. Its organizing questions include the identification of causal factors, the characterization of pathological mechanisms, and the development of interventions capable of preventing, modifying, or reversing those mechanisms. Modern biomedicine—the medical science that applies biological and physiological principles to clinical practice—is fundamentally organized around this orientation.

The historical development of cellular pathology, germ theory, molecular biology, pharmacology, and modern clinical sciences progressively established disease as an object that could be localized, characterized, measured, and targeted. Rudolf Virchow's cellular pathology shifted the conceptual focus of disease toward specific pathological changes in cells and tissues.2 The work of Pasteur, Koch, and others established microbial causes for important infectious diseases.3 Subsequent developments in physiology, biochemistry, genetics, and molecular biology progressively expanded the capacity to identify increasingly specific mechanisms of disease.

The institutional architecture of modern healthcare reflects this history. Differential diagnosis, laboratory medicine, imaging, pharmacology, surgery, clinical trials, and evidence-based medicine are all, in substantial measure, organized around identifying pathological processes and determining how those processes can be modified.

The achievements of this orientation are substantial and should not be minimized. Vaccination, antimicrobial therapy, surgical intervention, trauma care, and targeted molecular therapies have transformed morbidity and mortality across a wide range of conditions. The pathogenic orientation is particularly powerful when a relatively discrete pathological process can be identified and an intervention can be directed toward it with sufficient specificity. Yet this same orientation carries characteristic limits, taken up next.

The Limits of the Pathogenic Model

The case for salutogenesis is not a case against pathogenesis. It holds instead that pathogenesis, despite its extraordinary explanatory and therapeutic power, does not constitute a complete theory of health: it explains the loss of health—how disease arises, how it progresses, and how its mechanisms can be interrupted—but does not, by itself, explain why health is present, how it is maintained under continual perturbation, or why individuals exposed to similar challenges may experience markedly different trajectories.

This gap is not merely theoretical. The same institutional orientation that makes the pathogenic model so effective at diagnosing and treating discrete disease also makes it difficult to systematically represent phenomena that do not fit readily within a disease-centered framework. The limitation becomes increasingly apparent in chronic disease, multimorbidity, functional disorders, mental health, and other conditions in which pathophysiology is distributed across multiple interacting systems and unfolds over extended periods.

The strain is not limited to complex clinical presentations. The financial burden of pathogenesis-centered care has grown substantial, pricing an increasing number of patients out of the very systems built to treat them. Specialization, one of the pathogenic model's principal strengths, has simultaneously fragmented care into disconnected silos with limited structural integration, placing corresponding pressure on the relational and empathic dimensions of care for clinicians working within increasingly strained systems. In the United States in particular, healthcare—perhaps disease-care is the more precise term—increasingly exhibits signs of systemic strain.

One response holds that improved funding, administration, and data infrastructure, pursued within the existing pathogenic framework, would resolve these difficulties. This may be true of some of them. It is also worth asking whether the difficulties run deeper: whether the pathogenic model's own framing of health and disease is itself part of the problem, and whether traditional salutogenic systems, long set aside as scientifically premature, have something to contribute here, not through their specific vocabulary or remedies but through the underlying insight those systems were organized around.

In light of these limitations, the relevant clinical problem may not be reducible to the identification and elimination of a single pathological cause. The pathogenic question (what is wrong, how can it be treated?) remains necessary, but additional questions become relevant:

  • What regulatory capacities remain intact?
  • What processes are sustaining function despite disease?
  • What factors are impairing those processes?
  • What could strengthen the individual's capacity for adaptation and recovery?

These are salutogenic questions. They do not displace pathogenesis, but instead expand the clinical field of inquiry to include the complementary model examined next.

Salutogenesis: The Study of Health

The term salutogenesis was introduced by the medical sociologist Aaron Antonovsky in the 1970s.4 Antonovsky developed the concept in the context of his research into the relationship between stress, coping, and health, motivated by a fundamental question: why do some individuals maintain relatively good health despite exposure to substantial adversity and stress? Rather than beginning with the origins of disease, Antonovsky shifted attention toward the origins of health, emphasizing the role of psychosocial resources and, particularly, the sense of coherence: a global orientation built from three components—the experience of one's environment as comprehensible, manageable, and meaningful.

The instinct behind salutogenesis is far older than the word. Most of the world's traditional medical systems—Chinese medicine, Ayurveda, the Western humoral tradition—were already organized around supporting the body's own regulatory capacity centuries before biomedicine took up the pathogenic question with comparable rigor. Modern physiology has since developed an independent, evidence-based case for that same underlying capacity, spanning homeostasis, allostasis, psychoneuroimmunology, network physiology, and cellular bioelectricity.

Antonovsky developed and tested his model almost entirely within the psychosocial domain. The salutogenic question, however, need not be restricted to this domain. Health is often represented as a static state, defined by the absence of disease or by values falling within predefined physiological ranges; such definitions are useful for particular clinical and epidemiological purposes but capture only a partial description of the phenomenon. If health can be generated and sustained through psychosocial processes, it is reasonable to ask whether analogous processes operate on other levels of the human being.

A whole-person salutogenic model would extend Antonovsky's original sphere of observation across four dimensions of the individual organism, together with two further dimensions describing the context in which that organism is embedded:

  • Physical dimension: the body's material substance and structure—tissue, organ, and mineral composition—the level modern biomedicine already studies most directly.

  • Physiological dimension: not structure but the body's own regulatory, self-organizing activity, including thermoregulation, glucose regulation, cardiovascular control, immune regulation, inflammatory response, tissue repair, and neuroendocrine regulation.

  • Psychological dimension: emotional regulation, coping style, self-efficacy, and the appraisal processes captured by Antonovsky's sense of coherence.

  • Mental-spiritual dimension: belief systems, alignment with values, and a sense of purpose and meaning—the register toward which Antonovsky's own "meaningfulness" component gestures without fully entering.

  • Social dimension: social support, social integration, relational stability, and the broader social determinants of health, including socioeconomic status, education, and access to community resources.

  • Environmental/ecological dimension: the physical and built environment, exposure to environmental toxins, climate, and the broader ecological systems within which human health is embedded.

Each of the first four dimensions operates according to its own proper principles: physiological regulation, for instance, is described by homeostasis and allostasis, principles that do not transfer to the psychological or mental-spiritual register without translation. Later chapters in this project take up what that translation requires.

The Need for a Modern Salutogenic Framework

The contemporary movement toward integrative and whole-person medicine represents, in part, a response to the limitations of a strictly disease-centered orientation. Yet integration has frequently occurred through the addition of therapeutic modalities rather than through the synthesis of underlying conceptual frameworks: a biomedical model remains the primary organizing structure, while nutritional, botanical, behavioral, mind-body, traditional, or other interventions are incorporated alongside conventional treatment. Such an approach may be clinically valuable, but the presence of multiple therapeutic modalities does not by itself constitute integration at the level of theory.

Historical medical traditions provide important context for why that synthesis has proven difficult. Systems including Chinese medicine, Ayurveda, and Western humoral medicine developed extensive conceptual and clinical traditions concerned with maintaining balance, supporting vitality, and preserving health. These traditions should neither be idealized nor dismissed wholesale: their explanatory frameworks emerged within distinct historical, philosophical, and cultural contexts and cannot simply be transferred into contemporary medicine without critical examination, but the recurrence of health-oriented concepts across historically independent traditions raises a legitimate question, whether they repeatedly observed phenomena related to regulation, adaptation, and health maintenance that contemporary medicine is only now beginning to conceptualize through different scientific frameworks. The task, accordingly, is to identify the phenomena and practices each tradition describes and determine which can be meaningfully investigated through contemporary scientific methods—neither reducing every traditional concept to a preexisting biomedical mechanism nor replacing biomedicine with traditional medicine outright, but determining whether a broader conceptual framework can accommodate valid observations from multiple traditions while remaining compatible with rigorous empirical inquiry.

Accredited naturopathic medicine represents perhaps the clearest existing attempt to build such an integrated framework, and its limitations are instructive. Naturopathic curricula are nominally organized around an integrative philosophy, yet the core curriculum, textbooks, and clinical decision-support tools are drawn predominantly from biomedicine; salutogenic modalities such as hydrotherapy, botanical medicine, and homeopathy are typically incorporated alongside this biomedical core rather than integrated into a shared conceptual framework with it, and each retains its own separate following with limited systematic connection to the others or to biomedical mechanism. Practices sometimes grouped under "energy medicine" sit at the far end of this spectrum, oriented toward salutogenic goals but operating largely outside the evidentiary standards of contemporary biomedical science. That some naturopathic graduates pursue additional training in classical Chinese medicine or other traditional systems, often at considerable additional cost, suggests that the existing curriculum does not by itself supply an integrated salutogenic philosophy. This is not a criticism of naturopathic medicine so much as a symptom of the broader problem: no comprehensive, cross-traditional foundation comparable to biomedicine's currently exists for salutogenic practice.

Such a framework would need to explain not only disease mechanisms but also the processes through which health emerges and persists. That is the objective of a modern salutogenic medicine.

Shallow and Deep Salutogenesis

Developing such a framework requires examination at a level deeper than terminology. The fundamental differences between medical paradigms concern not only the concepts they use but also their assumptions about what exists (ontology) and how that reality can be known (epistemology). Ontology concerns the nature of reality: what constitutes a disease, what constitutes health, what constitutes an organism, and how biological systems relate to their environments. Epistemology concerns the nature and limits of knowledge: what constitutes evidence, how phenomena can be observed and measured, and which methods are considered capable of producing reliable knowledge.

These questions matter because a whole-person model can distinguish several levels of the human being, as the dimensions introduced above do, while still remaining committed to a single, materialist account of what is ultimately real. On such an account, the physical dimension is foundational, and the physiological, psychological, and mental-spiritual dimensions are built upward from it: emergent patterns of organization within an ultimately material substrate, describable, in principle without remainder, by the same kind of measurement used at the physical level. Multiplying dimensions in this way is a genuine advance over strict reductionism, since it takes seriously that health must be assessed at more than one level. But it does not by itself change what is assumed to be real or how that reality is assumed to be known. This is what might be called shallow salutogenesis: an approach that broadens the clinical picture across levels while remaining, ontologically and epistemologically, inside the framework of modern biomedicine.

Deep salutogenesis asks a more fundamental question of the same set of levels: whether the physiological, psychological, and mental-spiritual dimensions might be, in some meaningful sense, ontologically real in their own right, rather than convenient descriptive categories built up from a material base, each governed by laws proper to it and populated by real, non-material and non-physical objects and forces of its own. If so, a further question follows: what kind of knowing could actually reach such domains? Ordinary sensory measurement was built to grasp physical mechanism; a domain that is not physical may require something else, a mode of perception cultivated and refined beyond ordinary sense experience, capable of apprehending pattern, process, and meaning directly rather than only their physical residue.

This chapter does not attempt to settle that question; it states it clearly enough to be taken up properly. The difference between shallow and deep salutogenesis, on this account, is not a matter of degree, of one approach being more holistic than another, but a difference in kind, turning on whether the ontology and epistemology of biomedicine are merely extended across levels or genuinely reconsidered.

Towards an Integrative Medicine

Pathogenesis and salutogenesis are complementary orientations toward the same biological reality, not competing medical systems. Pathogenesis is indispensable when a specific pathology calls for targeted intervention; salutogenesis becomes increasingly relevant for prevention, chronic disease, recovery, and the restoration of a regulatory capacity that resists reduction to a single cause.

The distinction is between ways of conceptualizing the clinical problem, not between conventional and alternative medicine or between biomedical and traditional therapies: the same patient, the same intervention, and the same physiological system may all call for both kinds of reasoning at once.

The central clinical challenge, then, is not to determine which paradigm is correct, but which questions each paradigm is capable of answering—and how they can be brought into a coherent clinical framework. Operationalizing that judgment means locating, for a given intervention, which of the dimensions introduced earlier it actually acts on:

  • Physical: whether it acts on the body's material structure or substance—repairing tissue, correcting a deficiency, removing a lesion—the level at which the pathogenic model operates most directly.
  • Physiological: whether it supports the body's own regulatory activity, such as thermoregulation, immune function, or tissue repair, rather than substituting for it—the level at which pathogenic and salutogenic reasoning most directly meet.
  • Psychological: whether it acts on coping, emotional regulation, self-efficacy, or the appraisal processes captured by the sense of coherence.
  • Mental-spiritual: whether it engages belief, values, purpose, or meaning, rather than mechanism at any of the levels above.

An intervention may act on more than one of these levels at once, and evaluating its effects rigorously requires methods appropriate to whichever level, or levels, it actually engages.

The objective is not to construct a competing medicine that displaces biomedicine, but a more comprehensive one, capable of explaining both the processes through which health is lost and the processes through which it is generated, maintained, and restored: what causes disease, and what generates health.

The chapters that follow develop this framework progressively. The next chapter examines salutogenesis in greater depth, beginning with Antonovsky's original formulation and tracing the concept toward contemporary understandings of homeostasis, allostasis, physiological regulation, network physiology, and cellular bioelectricity, before taking up the distinction between shallow and deep salutogenesis introduced above in full: what an expanded, non-materialist ontology of the human being would actually claim, what kind of epistemology could support it, and why the difference between the two is a difference in kind rather than in degree.
