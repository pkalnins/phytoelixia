Conceptual Models in Medicine

Whether we are aware of them or not, conceptual models, or paradigms, form the lens and ways of thinking through which we come to understand the world within and around us. In science and medicine, these models contain the basic assumptions about what we think exists and what counts as meaningful observations. Thomas Kuhn's classic account of scientific paradigms captured exactly this: a paradigm functions as an entire way of seeing, determining in advance what will register as a relevant fact, what gets dismissed as noise, and what counts as legitimate work within the field at all2.

Throughout history, two primary conceptual models have shaped how we understand health and illness. The one most familiar is the pathogenic model, which starts from a disease and asks how to identify, explain, and eliminate it. This model forms the basis of modern medicine, or biomedicine, the medical science that applies biological and physiological principles to clinical practice. Its logic shapes what practitioners, researchers, students, healthcare administrators, and even the public are trained to notice: which problems get studied and funded, how students are taught to reason, and how diagnostic and billing codes get structured. (The next two sections below trace biomedicine's institutional foundations and historical development in full.)

A different model, the salutogenic model, starts from a different question: not what causes disease, but what generates and sustains health, and how that capacity can be strengthened directly so disease has less room to take root. On this view, health isn't simply the absence of disease, and disease is better understood as a loss of the body's own coherence than as an invading enemy to be defeated. Most of the world's older systems of medicine (Chinese medicine, Ayurveda, and the Western humoral tradition among them) are salutogenic in this sense, whether or not they used the word.

Most clinicians and patients alike, often without thinking about it, use the word "medicine" to mean only the pathogenic model. That's no accident: biomedicine built the full institutional apparatus of modern healthcare on top of it, from curricula and board exams to residency and hospital systems, insurance reimbursement, and the legal standard of care. And that association is well-earned. The pathogenic model has delivered a century of real gains in acute and infectious care, at a scale no salutogenic tradition can claim on its own.

Go a layer deeper and this preference has its own history. Biomedicine's methods carry the prestige of the Enlightenment's rational, empirical turn, and the common assumption is that biomedicine has superseded the older humoral traditions as a more primitive, prescientific stage that medicine has simply outgrown. Whatever those older systems got right, on this view, will eventually be validated, or discarded, by biomedicine's own methods, on biomedicine's own terms. This workbook is, in part, an argument against that assumption.

And yet, in real and increasingly visible ways, the pathogenic model is reaching its limits. It is well suited to a single named cause with a single targeted fix, and far less suited to chronic, multisystem, and mental health conditions that resist a single diagnosis or a single lesion. It is also expensive, in ways that are pricing an increasing number of people out of the very system built to treat them. Specialization, one of the pathogenic model's great strengths, has fragmented care into disconnected silos with little comprehensive integration, and the providers working inside that system are stretched thin enough that empathy is often the first casualty. In the United States especially, healthcare (perhaps disease-care is the more honest word) increasingly looks like a system under real strain.

Supporters of the pathogenic model will argue that better funding, better administration, more data, and further research within that same model will resolve all of this. That may be true of some of it. But it's also worth asking whether the issues run deeper: whether the pathogenic model's own way of framing health and disease is itself part of the problem, and whether the "quaint" traditional salutogenic systems it long ago set aside have something to offer here, not by borrowing their vocabulary or their specific remedies, but by taking seriously the core insight underneath them.

I believe both models are needed. The real question is how to integrate them well. There's a real resurgence of interest in integrative, "whole-person" healthcare right now, which is a genuinely good thing, but my own experience moving between the biomedical and complementary ("holistic") health worlds is that these two camps mostly stand side by side without touching. Practitioners in each speak their own language and often can't communicate meaningfully with one another; patients are left to guess which path to follow on their own; and the healthcare system itself, disease-care system really, offers almost no structural support for salutogenic practice.

Salutogenic medicine also suffers from a real legitimacy gap, and not without reason. Its traditional systems are self-contained: developed centuries apart, in different languages, on different premises, with no shared modern vocabulary connecting them to each other or to contemporary physiology. There is no single comprehensive foundation, comparable to biomedicine's, that a salutogenic practitioner could be trained in the way a physician is trained in biomedicine, and no shared practice guidelines or clinical decision-support tools outside any one traditional lineage.

What's needed is a modern salutogenic medicine that extends the foundations biomedicine is built on rather than replacing them. That's what this workbook is attempting: an outline for a more comprehensive medicine, one that fully integrates both models, in the hope of building something closer to real healthcare rather than only disease-care, a framework that knows when each model belongs and applies it there.

But full integration takes more than reorganizing existing ideas or borrowing new vocabulary. It requires going down to the ontology (what is real) and epistemology (how we come to know it) underneath both models, as each developed historically. Traditional salutogenic medicine's ontology and epistemology are fundamentally different from modern biomedicine's, and simply swapping concepts from one paradigm into the other risks losing exactly what made the original insight work. I'd argue that's what a lot of today's integrative and whole-person medicine is actually doing. This workbook tries to go deeper than that.

The rest of this chapter takes up both models at the level of basic definitions: what each claims, and where each came from historically. Later chapters in this foundations series go much deeper, especially the very next one, "Salutogenesis," which traces the concept's full modern grounding, from Antonovsky's original psychosocial insight through homeostasis, allostasis, network physiology, and cellular bioelectricity, out to one real clinical attempt at practicing it, before taking up the shallow/deep distinction and the ontology and epistemology it turns on.

Pathogenesis: The Study of Disease

Pathogenesis is biomedicine's organizing question: how does a specific disease state arise, by what mechanism does it unfold, and how is it eliminated? It is a question about the pathogen, the lesion, the mutation, the deficiency: some identifiable, nameable cause, traced from origin through mechanism to intervention. In Aristotle's terms, this is a search for material cause almost exclusively: what a thing is physically made of, and what physically brought its current state about, treating the other three causes he named as beside the point. (The foundations chapter "Salutogenesis" takes up Aristotle's full four-cause framework and what the other three causes restore.)

Modern biomedicine's institutional shape follows directly from taking this question as primary: Rudolf Virchow's cellular pathology relocated disease to a specific, visible site in the tissue rather than a systemic humoral imbalance1; the germ theory of Pasteur and Koch gave that lesion a discrete external cause; and the whole apparatus of differential diagnosis, targeted pharmacology, and evidence-based medicine that followed is built to answer exactly one kind of question well: what is wrong, specifically, and what removes it. (The foundations chapter "Worldviews" traces this history and its philosophical commitments in full.)

The success of this approach is not in serious dispute. Antibiotics, vaccination, surgery, trauma care, and targeted molecular therapeutics have done more to reduce suffering and extend life than any preceding medical tradition, and they did so specifically because pathogenesis asks a narrow, mechanistic, falsifiable question rather than a diffuse one. A clinician who cannot name what is wrong, cannot quantify it, cannot reliably fix it. Nothing that follows here is an argument against that discipline. It is an argument that pathogenesis is a necessary but incomplete question, because it is built to explain disease and structurally has very little to say about health.

Salutogenesis: The Study of Health

Salutogenesis is the deliberate mirror of that question: not what causes disease, but what generates and sustains health, so that capacity can be strengthened directly rather than only defended once a specific cause is found. The medical sociologist Aaron Antonovsky coined the term in the 1970s, arriving at it while studying how differently Holocaust survivors adjusted to menopause: a meaningful fraction of the women who had survived the deepest imaginable pathogenic assault remained, by any reasonable standard, well, and Antonovsky spent the following two decades trying to name what let them.

The instinct behind salutogenesis is far older than the word. Most of the world's traditional medical systems, Chinese medicine, Ayurveda, the Western humoral tradition, were already organized around supporting the body's own regulatory capacity centuries before biomedicine took up the pathogenic question with comparable rigor. Modern physiology has since built its own independent, evidence-grounded case for that same underlying capacity, from homeostasis and allostasis through psychoneuroimmunology to network physiology and cellular bioelectricity. (The next foundations chapter "Salutogenesis" takes up that whole arc in full, along with a distinction this page doesn't have room for: not all salutogenic medicine, including a great deal of what currently passes for "whole-person" or integrative care, turns out to be equally salutogenic.)

Conclusion

Pathogenesis and salutogenesis are not competitors for the same territory. Pathogenesis is indispensable wherever a rapid, targeted intervention against a specific, identifiable cause is what saves the patient: trauma, acute infection, a structural failure that needs mechanical repair. Salutogenesis is indispensable everywhere pathogenesis reaches its limit: chronic disease, prevention, resilience, the slow accumulation of dysfunction that has no single identifiable cause to eliminate. They are two different tools suited to two different classes of clinical problem, and a mature medicine needs both, practiced together rather than side by side.

That integration is this workbook's actual project, and it turns out to require more than borrowing a more holistic-sounding vocabulary. The next foundations chapter "Salutogenesis" takes up the concept in full: its modern physiological grounding, and a distinction between a shallow salutogenesis still working inside biomedicine's own materialist assumptions and a deeper one that expands them, that most integrative medicine today never draws.
