An opinion piece.

The Foundations overview traced pathogenesis and salutogenesis at the level of basic definitions: two different questions medicine can organize itself around, and why a mature medicine needs both rather than one. Anthroposophically-extended medicine (AEM) occupies an interesting position between them. It does not discard the pathogenic apparatus of modern medicine, the diagnostics, the acute interventions, the pharmacology, as somehow false or obsolete. It treats that apparatus as necessary but incomplete, and builds a salutogenic layer on top of it rather than in place of it. This chapter takes up that salutogenic layer in full: where the concept comes from, what grounds it physiologically, and, the part that will be less comfortable for some readers, why most of what currently passes for integrative or "whole-person" medicine is only half the story.

Salutogenesis's Modern Grounding

Start with where the concept comes from, and how far modern science has independently traveled toward the same conclusion.

Antonovsky's Question

The medical sociologist Aaron Antonovsky coined salutogenesis (literally, the origin of health) as the deliberate mirror image of pathogenesis: not what causes disease, but what causes health17. He arrived at the question almost by accident. In a 1970 study of how Israeli women of different ethnic backgrounds adjusted to menopause, one subgroup was made up of survivors of Nazi concentration camps. Antonovsky expected, reasonably, that this group would show uniformly poor health outcomes. Instead, he found that 29% of the survivors rated in good emotional health, a meaningful fraction, though well below the 51% of the comparison group who had not lived through the camps18. The gap mattered. But so did the fact that it wasn't zero. Something had let more than a quarter of women who had survived the deepest imaginable pathogenic assault remain, by any reasonable standard, well.

Antonovsky spent the following two decades trying to name that something. His answer, developed fully in Unraveling the Mystery of Health (1987), reframed illness and wellness not as a binary but as a continuum (the "health ease/dis-ease" continuum) that every person occupies somewhere on, at every moment, under constant pressure from what he called generalized resistance resources: money, knowledge, social support, cultural stability, ego identity, and more, each of which helps an organism absorb stressors without being structurally damaged by them17. The construct that did the most explanatory work, though, was what he named the sense of coherence (SOC): a global orientation toward one's world built from three components: comprehensibility (the world makes cognitive sense), manageability (one has the resources to meet its demands), and meaningfulness (its demands are worth engaging with, not just endured)19. A strong sense of coherence, on Antonovsky's model, is not a claim that a person won't be exposed to pathogens or stressors. The concentration camp survivors were exposed to about as much as a human being can survive. It is a claim about what determines whether exposure becomes damage.

From the Psychosocial to the Physiological

Antonovsky built and tested his model almost entirely in the psychosocial domain: coping, stress appraisal, mental and emotional health outcomes. This book's central wager is that the same underlying logic (that there exists an organism-level capacity for maintaining order, function, and integrity under perturbation, separable from and prior to the question of what perturbs it) is not a fact about psychology specifically. It is a fact about living systems, and it applies with equal force at the physiological level: to what allows a cell, a tissue, an organ, an organ network to keep functioning coherently under metabolic, immunological, and structural load, not only what allows a mind to keep functioning under psychosocial load.

This is, in fact, closer to how most of the world's traditional medical systems already worked, long before Antonovsky gave the underlying idea a name. Classical Chinese medicine's whole apparatus of Qi, Yang, Yin, and Blood, the very vocabulary this book builds on, was never organized primarily around naming external pathogens (though it has a vocabulary for those too); it was organized around reading and supporting the body's own regulatory capacity, using constitution, pattern, and context rather than a fixed disease entity as the unit of diagnosis. The Western vitalist and eclectic/naturopathic traditions named this capacity outright, as the vis medicatrix naturae (the healing power of nature), and built their therapeutics around supporting it rather than around suppressing a named pathological process. Ayurveda's prakriti/vikriti framework and its emphasis on restoring dosha balance runs on the same underlying logic. None of these traditions used the word "salutogenic" (the word didn't exist yet), but structurally, they were physiologic-salutogenic medicine, asking what maintains the organism's own order, centuries before biomedicine asked the pathogenic question with comparable rigor.

Homeostasis and Allostasis

Western physiology has its own lineage for this same idea, developed independently of both Antonovsky and the traditional systems above, and it is this lineage, not Antonovsky's own psychosocial framework directly, that gives this book's physiological salutogenesis its most direct modern scientific footing.

Claude Bernard first proposed, in 1865, that the stability of the milieu intérieur (the internal fluid environment bathing every cell) was the precondition for what he called "free and independent life": an organism is only as free as its ability to keep its interior constant despite a changing exterior20. Walter Cannon gave that stability a name, homeostasis, and an operational description: the coordinated physiological processes, spanning the nervous system, the endocrine glands, and the viscera, that hold the organism's steady states within survivable range21. Classical homeostasis is a fixed-setpoint model: a variable (temperature, pH, glucose) is defended around a stable target through negative feedback.

Later physiology extended this considerably. Allostasis (literally, "stability through change") was proposed by Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer to describe something homeostasis alone does not capture: the organism doesn't just defend fixed setpoints, it actively shifts them in anticipation of demand, raising blood pressure before waking, mobilizing cortisol before a stressor even arrives, because anticipating and adapting is more efficient than only reacting after the fact22. Bruce McEwen and Eliot Stellar then named the cost side of this same capacity: allostatic load, the cumulative physiological wear that results when allostatic systems are activated too often, too intensely, or for too long, or fail to shut off once the demand has passed2324. Read together, homeostasis and allostasis are a rigorous, quantifiable, thoroughly biomedical description of exactly the capacity Antonovsky was pointing at with the sense of coherence, except measured in cortisol curves and autonomic tone rather than questionnaires, and describing tissue and organ systems rather than a person's global orientation to their world. It is the missing physiological half of his psychosocial model, arrived at from the opposite direction.

Extending the Model: Neuroendocrine Immunology, Systems Biology, and Network Physiology

None of this stayed confined to endocrinology. Robert Ader and Nicholas Cohen's 1975 discovery that immune suppression could be behaviorally conditioned (by pairing an immunosuppressive drug with a neutral taste, then eliciting the same immune effect from the taste alone) demonstrated directly that the nervous system, and by extension the psychological state of the organism, could causally drive immune function, not merely correlate with it25. That single finding launched psychoneuroimmunology, the field mapping the bidirectional signaling between nervous, endocrine, and immune systems26, and it closed the loop this book needs closed: Antonovsky's sense of coherence is not a metaphor sitting alongside the body's physiology. It has direct, mechanistic, measurable neuroendocrine-immune correlates. A "meaningful, manageable, comprehensible" appraisal of one's circumstances is not just a mental state; it is a pattern of autonomic tone, HPA-axis activity, and cytokine signaling with real downstream physiological consequences, which is exactly the kind of claim this book makes, organ by organ, when it maps Yang/Qi/Blood/Yin activity onto autonomic, inflammatory, and structural pathways.

Two further scientific developments give this same logic its full expression at the level of the whole organism rather than one axis at a time. General systems theory, formalized by Ludwig von Bertalanffy, argued that living organisms are open systems best understood through their organization and feedback dynamics as an integrated whole, not by reducing them to isolated parts studied in sequence27. This is the same organism-as-integrated-whole commitment this book's twelve-organ framework depends on. And network physiology, developed most systematically by Plamen Ivanov and colleagues, gives that commitment a direct, contemporary experimental method: mapping how distinct organ systems (cardiac, respiratory, autonomic, and beyond) dynamically couple and decouple from one another to produce a given physiological state, with the specific topology of those interactions, not any single organ in isolation, determining function and, ultimately, health2829. This is the same organ-network logic this book's own "Twelve Organ Processes" module builds on, and the same reason each organ chapter traces its Yang/Qi/Blood/Yin activity outward into that organ's relationships with the others: an axis, in this book's sense, is a network-physiology edge with a name and a clinical history behind it.

Bioelectricity and Morphospace: Levin's Extension

Michael Levin's research program pushes the network picture down to the level of the individual cell's own information processing, and gives the salutogenic idea, an organism-level drive toward a correct, self-maintaining configuration, its most direct experimental demonstration yet. Levin's central finding is that cells coordinate anatomical outcomes not only through gene expression and diffusible chemical signals but through bioelectric signaling, patterns of transmembrane voltage, propagated cell-to-cell through gap junctions, functioning as a distinct, "software"-like information layer sitting on top of the "hardware" of the genome30. That layer encodes what Levin calls a target morphology: not just what a tissue currently is, but what shape the collective of cells is oriented toward becoming or returning to, a target that can be read out, and even rewritten, by changing bioelectric state directly, without touching a single gene.

The clearest demonstrations come from planarian flatworms, which normally regenerate a single head after being cut in two. Briefly blocking the gap junctions that let cells share bioelectric signal, with a chemical, not a genetic edit, can permanently reset a worm's target morphology so that it, and its cell lineage, goes on regenerating two heads indefinitely, long after the original treatment has washed out of the tissue4; the same style of manipulation can even induce a flatworm to regenerate the head shape characteristic of an entirely different species, again with a completely unaltered genome31. The anatomical outcome, in other words, is stored and reprogrammable as a bioelectric pattern, a kind of pattern memory the tissue holds about what shape it belongs in, sitting alongside, not reducible to, the genome. Levin frames the full range of possible anatomical configurations a collective of cells could settle into as a morphospace, with development, regeneration, and even cancer suppression all describable as the process of navigating that space toward, or away from, a stable, correct attractor30.

This gives this book's salutogenic framing about as direct a physical mechanism as currently exists in the literature: an organism does not merely sustain damage and then get rebuilt piece by piece from outside. It holds an active, distributed, rewritable representation of its own correct configuration, and moves back toward that configuration given the right conditions, which reads as close to a literal biophysical restatement of the vis medicatrix naturae. It also sharpens what "supporting the body's own healing capacity" can mean at the mechanistic level: not simply removing an obstacle and waiting, but actively shifting the system's regulatory conditions (bioelectric, and, this book argues, energetic/etheric) toward the state in which its own self-organizing capacity can complete the work itself.

Functional Medicine: One Attempt at Network Thinking in the Clinic

Network physiology and bioelectric morphospace are basic science: they describe how the organism actually works. Translating that picture into an actual clinical practice, at the bedside, is a separate and much harder problem, and biomedicine-adjacent practice has already made one serious, large-scale attempt at it: functional medicine, which biochemist Jeffrey Bland and his wife Susan founded through the Institute for Functional Medicine in 1991, built explicitly on a systems-biology premise rather than the single-disease, single-target model pathogenesis-oriented medicine defaults to32. Its organizing move is to treat the body's major regulatory systems (assimilation, defense and repair, energy production, biotransformation and elimination, transport, communication, and structural integrity) as an interconnected network rather than a set of independently run organ specialties, and to look for the upstream, root-level imbalance connecting a patient's symptoms across systems rather than treating each downstream symptom as its own isolated problem.

That ambition is directly continuous with everything else in this section: it is, in effect, an attempt to build pathogenesis-era clinical infrastructure (careful differential workup, laboratory-verifiable findings, individualized treatment) on top of a salutogenic, network-level premise about what health actually is. In the same spirit of honesty this book applied to pathogenesis's own real successes, it is also fair to note that functional medicine draws real and repeated criticism for outrunning its evidence base: many specific functional-medicine protocols and supplement interventions have not been validated in rigorous trials, and the field remains largely unregulated and inconsistently practiced from clinic to clinic. That critique of particular protocols is not a refutation of the underlying premise. That the body's organ systems form an interconnected network, and that root-cause, whole-system thinking is a legitimate complement to single-target diagnosis, is exactly what network physiology demonstrates experimentally, independent of any one clinical movement's track record. This book can be read as another attempt at that same underlying project, using classical Chinese pattern differentiation, Western herbalism, and anthroposophic physiology as its own working vocabulary for the network functional medicine is also trying to describe.

Taken together, this is the arc traced above in full: from Antonovsky's original psychosocial question, through the physiological tradition, old and new, that answers the same question about the body itself, through the cellular-level evidence that an organism actively navigates back toward its own correct configuration, to the clinical attempts already underway to put network-level thinking into practice at the bedside. The organ-network framework the rest of this book is built on belongs to that same lineage.

All of this (Antonovsky's model, the physiological lineage from homeostasis through network physiology, Levin's bioelectric work, functional medicine's clinical attempt) is real, rigorous, and evidence-grounded. But what I want to argue now is that not all salutogenic medicine is equally salutogenic. There is a shallow version and a deep version, and the difference between them is not a matter of degree (more holistic, less holistic) but a difference in kind, turning on a distinction most people, including most practitioners, never explicitly draw: the distinction between a science's ontology and its epistemology.

Two Layers of Science

Science is both a model of what is real (its ontology) and a method for coming to know reality (its epistemology). Most people conflate the two, and mostly for good reason: they identify "science" with its epistemology — the scientific method, hypothesis and experiment, the discipline of testing a claim against the world rather than resting on authority or personal opinion — and they are right to hold that method in high regard. It is, after all, one of the most reliable error-correcting procedures human beings have ever devised.

But many people, including — and this is the uncomfortable part — many practitioners within the integrative and complementary medicine community, also unconsciously adopt the ontological commitments that have historically traveled alongside that method: not because the method requires them, but because they were never taught to distinguish the method from the metaphysics riding on top of it. That ontology has a name: metaphysical naturalism.

Metaphysical naturalism arose out of the Scientific Revolution in Western Europe, roughly from Copernicus's 1543 De Revolutionibus1 through Newton's 1687 Principia2. Its philosophical center of gravity is René Descartes (1596–1650), whose split between res cogitans (thinking substance) and res extensa (extended substance)3 first isolated the observing mind from a purely mechanical external nature — a move I'll return to below as the origin of what I'll call spectator consciousness. Metaphysical naturalism itself is the later, more radical descendant of that split: the assertion that physical matter and blind physical forces (gravity, electromagnetism, the nuclear interactions) are the whole of what exists, full stop, including inside the mind that first drew the boundary.

Unpacked, metaphysical naturalism commits its adherents to several linked claims:

  • Physicalism. There are no "vital" or otherwise special forces governing life apart from ordinary physical and chemical ones. Molecular biology and biochemistry are, in principle, fully adequate to explain life; life simply emerges from physical-chemical processes, by some mechanism not yet understood in detail. Organisms are elaborate machines, ultimately obeying the same mineral-level laws as everything else.
  • No causally efficacious consciousness. Consciousness arises from neural activity, which in turn reduces to molecular biology and genetics — the brain is, on this view, a kind of biological computer. How, exactly, remains the notorious "hard problem of consciousness," but the working assumption is that a sufficiently detailed account of the hardware will eventually dissolve the mystery.
  • No free will, no teleology. Purpose and agency, in biology as in psychology, are treated as folk illusions generated by, but not causally operative in, the underlying physical process.
  • Reductionism. The whole is nothing more than the sum of its parts. Living organisms — and consciousness along with them — are best understood by dissecting activity down to the molecular level and reassembling the explanation from there.
  • Mechanism. The activities of life and mind run on physical-chemical mechanisms; the human being just is a very complex machine.

There is a further question metaphysical naturalism never quite settles cleanly: are the mathematical laws we discover in nature literal features of a mind-independent reality, or are they human constructs — useful, predictively powerful maps that we should not mistake for the territory? Working scientists disagree with each other on this point more than the popular image of a unified "scientific worldview" would suggest, and I don't think naturalism as such forces an answer either way. I raise it mainly to flag that the ontology is less monolithic, and less obviously "just what science says," than it's usually presented as being.

That last observation matters more than it looks. Metaphysical naturalism is not itself a testable hypothesis. The scientific method can test claims about physical mechanisms; it cannot test the prior claim that physical mechanisms are the whole of reality, because that claim isn't a physical, empirically adjudicable claim in the first place — it's a metaphysical stance about what a physical finding is allowed to mean. It was simply adopted, as a working assumption, during the Scientific Revolution, and it has been carried forward ever since as though it were itself an empirical result rather than an interpretive lens. Most people who use it don't realize this distinction exists. I'd go further and argue that metaphysical naturalism arises out of a particular, historically conditioned mode of human consciousness — what I'll call, following the Cartesian split above, spectator consciousness: the stance of a mind that has withdrawn behind a pane of glass to observe a world it no longer considers itself part of.

Shallow Salutogenesis

This brings me to what I mean by shallow salutogenesis. I'd place a great deal of contemporary "whole-person" medicine here: functional medicine, systems-biology-informed integrative care, and — this is the part that will be less comfortable for some readers — a great deal of what gets called "energy medicine."

What makes a salutogenic approach shallow, on my definition, is not a lack of sophistication or a lack of genuine clinical value. It's that the approach remains grounded in the ontology and epistemology of modern biomedicine — metaphysical naturalism, and the measurement of physical quantities as the sole valid form of empirical knowledge — even while it reaches for a more integrative clinical picture.

To be fair to this work: whole-person medicine, even though it divides the human being into different levels of activity, is a genuine advance over strict reductionism, and functional medicine in particular can yield real insight into chronic disease that pathogenic models miss entirely. Systems biology and network physiology — treating the organism as an interacting network rather than a bag of independent parts — are the logical next step in medicine's own internal evolution, and I don't think that evolution is a bad thing. These are all legitimately salutogenic models. They are just still operating inside metaphysical naturalism's ontology, which sets a ceiling on how far they can go.

The ceiling becomes visible at exactly the point where practitioners start reaching for words like energy, soul, or spirit — because they reach for those words from inside metaphysical naturalism's frame, not outside it. And from inside that frame, there is only one category available for anything that isn't ordinary matter: energy, conceived on the model of physics — a scalar or vector quantity, in principle measurable, that does work. So life, consciousness, and spirit — phenomena that I'd argue are genuinely higher-order, that very plausibly obey their own laws and occupy their own "spaces" independent of, and not reducible to, physical space-time — get quietly flattened into that one available category. This is a category error, and it is the single most consistent tell of shallow salutogenesis.

Two examples make the error concrete.

Michael Levin's work on cellular bioelectricity, discussed above, is the first. A morphospace is not itself an energy, even though it is expressed via measurable bioelectric voltage gradients. It's an abstract space of possible anatomical configurations that the organism's activity moves through, under something like a teleological pull toward a target state. Popular accounts routinely collapse this into "bioelectric energy," and in doing so, lose the very thing that makes Levin's work important: that it points to organizing information, not a subtler fuel.

The same error recurs one level up with psychological "space." A person's inner emotional and cognitive life — the structure of a Jungian complex, the geometry of what psychologists sometimes model as semantic or affective "space" — is not physical space, and treating "psychic energy" as a literal, if subtler, electromagnetic-style quantity makes the identical mistake Levin's popularizers make, just applied to mind instead of tissue.

Each of these domains — the etheric, if you like, and the astral — has its own proper laws, and lies genuinely outside the bounds of what "energy" means in physics. They are not attenuated, high-frequency, or otherwise "subtle" forms of energy. They are, in kind, beyond energy altogether. Shallow salutogenesis is what results when a practitioner senses this — correctly — but has no vocabulary available to name it except the one metaphysical naturalism supplies.

So: what's the alternative?

Deep Salutogenesis

This is, I think, exactly the place where Rudolf Steiner (1861–1925) has been most persistently misunderstood — not only by his critics, but by many of his own followers, and by practitioners of anthroposophically-extended medicine who import his vocabulary of "etheric" and "astral" forces back into a materialist frame rather than the idealist one he actually intended. Calling something "etheric energy" doesn't rescue the concept from the category error above; it just relocates the same error one level deeper into the tradition that was supposed to be the alternative to it.

Getting this right requires two separate expansions — one ontological, one epistemological — and deep salutogenesis, as I use the term, is what you get only when both are made together.

Expanding the Ontology: From Materialism to Idealism

The ontological move is to expand metaphysical naturalism into Idealism — and I mean a rigorous, contemporary formulation such as Bernardo Kastrup's (b. 1974) Analytic Idealism5, not a vague spiritual gesture. On this view, thought — mind, or consciousness itself — rather than matter, is the ground of the world. Matter is not "dead stuff" that somehow generates mind; matter is how mental, ideational activity appears when viewed from outside, through an external sensory apparatus. A living organism, on this reading, is something like a localized whirlpool in a single underlying stream of consciousness, not an assembly of parts that mind gets added to afterward.

Expanding the Epistemology: The Phenomenological Method

Ontology alone changes nothing clinically; it has to be paired with an epistemology capable of actually reaching the new territory it opens up. That epistemology is the phenomenological method originated by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe (1749–1832)6 and systematized by Steiner into an explicit ladder of cognitive modes.7 Where the ordinary scientific epistemology of measurement is built to grasp mechanism, phenomenology is built to grasp gesture, pattern, and — at its furthest reach — thought itself, directly, rather than only its physical residue.

Applied level by level, this looks like:

  • On the level of life, one moves beyond quantifying molecules and isolated pathways to perceiving polarities, patterns, and processes unfolding in time — an organ's or organism's characteristic gesture rather than its parts list. Steiner termed this relational mode of cognition Imagination, and it discloses real phenomena that purely intellectual, discursive knowing cannot reach at all, not merely reaches less efficiently: the etheric formative forces actually at work in a living process — the gestures of expansion and contraction, dissolving and hardening, that shape an organism through time.
  • On the level of consciousness, what Steiner called Inspiration allows direct perception of psychological processes, patterns, and archetypes — territory Carl Jung (1875–1961) arrived at independently, and partially, through his own concept of the collective unconscious and its archetypes.8
  • At a further reach still, Intuition allows one to grasp purpose, meaning, and one's own connection to activities and forces beyond the individual self — the point at which the separate gestures Imagination perceives and the patterns Inspiration perceives resolve into a single overarching archetype. Goethe's Urpflanze — the one archetypal plant-gesture underlying all of vegetation — belongs here, not at the Imaginative stage: it is not a further, more detailed gesture among others, but the unifying blueprint all those gestures were already expressing.

Cultivated through sustained practice and inner effort, these modes of knowing let a person perceive dimensions of reality that simply are not contained within ordinary physical space-time. Steiner, drawing on older esoteric and occult teachings, named three such dimensions: the etheric — the information-space holding and directing life-activity, which then projects into physical process; the astral — the information-space holding psychic and soul activity; and the devachanic — the information-space of pure thought or spirit itself.9

Beyond Energy: How the Subtle Shapes the Physical

Here is the crucial move that distinguishes deep salutogenesis from the shallow version, and that I think even many committed anthroposophic practitioners get wrong: these are spaces that interact with the physical world not through physical energies, but through something closer to phase alignment or resonance. The information held in the etheric, astral, and devachanic does not add energy to the physical system; it shapes physical matter and energy — including, for instance, the very bioelectric gradients Levin maps — as a formative template, without being identical to, or reducible to, that physical activity.

This is precisely the distinction that dissolves the category error. Practitioners in integrative medicine who speak of "energy" — the felt sensation of tingling or pressure while lightly scanning a patient's field, say — are attempting to frame a genuine, subtle perception using the only vocabulary metaphysical naturalism makes available. Physical energies are very likely involved as a downstream correlate. But they are being guided and formed by something more subtle and more fundamental than themselves; the pattern is primary, and the energetic trace is secondary, a shadow the pattern casts into physical space-time, not the thing itself.

The morphogenetic field is a good test case, because the term has real history rather than being purely esoteric coinage: it entered developmental biology in the early twentieth century (Alexander Gurwitsch's (1874–1954) "embryonic field" concept10), well before Rupert Sheldrake gave it a far more speculative reinterpretation as "morphic resonance" in the 1980s.11 What I want to insist on is the original, more modest and more defensible claim buried in the concept: this field is not itself an energy field, and not electromagnetic. It can shape electromagnetic fields in the physical body — bioelectric gradients among them — without being located in physical space at all. Steiner gestured toward this as counterspace, a notion his students George Adams (1894–1963) and Olive Whicher later worked out rigorously using projective geometry.12 It interacts with matter through non-energetic means I've explored at greater length elsewhere14 — phase alignment, and possibly the magnetic vector potential, are candidate physical correlates, though this remains genuinely speculative territory rather than settled science.

Astral fields — the basis of emotional and soul-level patterning — work the same way, and behave less like a fluid or a charge than like a phase field in physics or systems biology: they have topological structure, and something like attractor basins, in the sense C. H. Waddington (1905–1975) meant when he described development running down channels in an "epigenetic landscape."13 That image, notably, is itself a precursor to Levin's morphospaces — the same underlying intuition, arrived at from a different direction, decades earlier.

The Fourfold Human Being, and Why Modern Medicine Never Leaves Earth

Steiner laid out the fourfold human being on the basis of directly perceiving four distinct kinds of formative activity within physical matter, each governed by its own proper laws — activities we can label, for convenience, with the names of the classical elements: Earth, Water, Air, Fire. Modern biomedicine, and by direct extension every "shallow" salutogenic therapy built on its ontology, never actually leaves the Earth level: the level of fixed, measurable, mineral structure — even when its ostensible subject is life, feeling, or spirit.

Aristotle's Four Causes, Recovered

There is an older, and I think clarifying, vocabulary for the same diagnosis. Aristotle held that a complete answer to "why is this thing the way it is?" requires four distinct kinds of explanation, not one: the material cause (what a thing is made of), the formal cause (the pattern or organization that makes it the specific kind of thing it is), the efficient cause (the source of the change that brought it about), and the final cause (the end or purpose for the sake of which it exists).15

Modern biology and modern medicine, for all their sophistication, have narrowed this fourfold inheritance down to one member of the set. They answer "why" almost exclusively in terms of material cause — the molecules, the genes, the biochemical substrate a thing happens to be built from — and treat the other three either as eliminable in principle or as loose figures of speech for material processes not yet fully worked out. This is, in effect, the same reduction to "Earth" described above, restated in Aristotle's own terms.

The fourfold human being, read this way, restores exactly the causal structure modern science has narrowed away, one level at a time:

Aristotelian Cause Steiner's Body What It Explains
Material cause Physical The molecules, tissues, and mineral substance biomedicine already studies competently, and, under metaphysical naturalism, the only level it recognizes as real at all.
Efficient cause Etheric Aristotle's efficient cause is the primary source of a change — what actually brings it about. The etheric body is, quite literally, this: the ongoing formative activity, the "software" described earlier, that actively drives growth, regeneration, and repair moment to moment, without which physical process would have no direction at all.
Formal cause Astral Aristotle's own considered view, in De Anima, is that the soul just is the formal cause of a living body — not an extra ingredient added to matter from outside, but the very organization that makes a body alive and sentient rather than a mere assemblage of parts.16 Loosely mapped onto Steiner's more differentiated scheme, this is the office of the astral: what gives an organism its specific ensouled "kind," the particular form of sentient organization that makes it the kind of experiencing being it is.
Final cause Devachanic Purpose and meaning as such — "that for the sake of which," in Aristotle's own phrase — is exactly the register Intuition, described above, is built to grasp.

Modern medicine's blindness to three-quarters of Aristotle's own causal scheme is not a modern discovery superseding an ancient error. It is a modern narrowing, mistaken for progress.

Conclusion: Expanding Both, Not Either

Deep salutogenesis, as I'm using the term, is not simply salutogenic medicine with more spiritual language layered on top. It requires expanding medicine's ontology, from metaphysical naturalism to something like Idealism, so that life, soul, and spirit are recognized as genuinely real domains with their own laws rather than exotic byproducts of matter awaiting a future physical explanation. And it requires expanding medicine's epistemology in step, from measurement alone to the full phenomenological path — Imagination, Inspiration, Intuition — so that practitioners have organs of perception actually adequate to the reality the expanded ontology says is there.

Neither expansion alone is sufficient. An ontology without a matching epistemology is just an unverifiable metaphysical claim, no better founded than the materialism it replaces. An epistemology without a matching ontology is what produces shallow salutogenesis: real, disciplined, gesture-based perception, forced back into a vocabulary — "energy" — that was never built to hold it. Only when both move together do you get a medicine capable of treating the whole human being on each of that being's own proper terms, rather than translating three-quarters of the patient down into the vocabulary of the fourth.

Salutogenesis's Modern Grounding

  • The Foundations overview covers pathogenesis/salutogenesis at the level of basic definitions; this chapter takes up salutogenesis in full. Anthroposophically-extended medicine (AEM) keeps biomedicine's pathogenic apparatus (diagnostics, acute intervention, pharmacology) and adds a salutogenic layer on top, treating it as necessary but incomplete rather than obsolete.
  • Aaron Antonovsky coined salutogenesis (the origin of health) as the deliberate mirror of pathogenesis: not what causes disease, but what causes health. He arrived at the question studying Holocaust survivors' menopause adjustment (1970): 29% rated in good emotional health despite the deepest imaginable pathogenic assault, below the 51% comparison group, but not zero.
  • His sense of coherence (comprehensibility, manageability, meaningfulness) is backed by generalized resistance resources (money, knowledge, social support, etc.) that let an organism absorb stressors without structural damage. Not a claim that stressor exposure is avoided; a claim about what determines whether exposure becomes damage.
  • Antonovsky's model was psychosocial; this book's wager is that the same underlying capacity (order/function/integrity under perturbation) applies with equal force physiologically, to cells, tissues, organs, organ networks.
  • Traditional medicine got there first: Chinese medicine's Qi/Yang/Yin/Blood, Western vitalism's vis medicatrix naturae, Ayurveda's prakriti/vikriti, all organized around supporting the body's own regulatory capacity centuries before biomedicine took disease seriously with comparable rigor.
  • Western physiology built its own independent case: Claude Bernard's milieu intérieur (1865) → Walter Cannon's homeostasis (fixed-setpoint negative feedback) → Sterling/Eyer's allostasis (setpoints actively shift in anticipation of demand) → McEwen/Stellar's allostatic load (the wear from over-activated or never-shut-off allostatic systems).
  • Ader/Cohen's 1975 conditioned-immunosuppression finding launched psychoneuroimmunology: sense of coherence has direct neuroendocrine-immune correlates, not just a mental-state analogy.
  • Von Bertalanffy's general systems theory and Ivanov's network physiology (organ systems dynamically coupling/decoupling, topology determining function) extend the same logic to the whole organism, the same premise this book's organ-network framework depends on.
  • Michael Levin: cells coordinate anatomy through bioelectric signaling, a "software" layer encoding a target morphology. Planarian flatworms: blocking gap junctions can permanently reset target morphology to regenerate two heads, or another species' head shape, with an unaltered genome. The anatomical outcome is a rewritable bioelectric pattern, not reducible to genome; Levin frames this as navigating a morphospace.
  • Functional medicine (Jeffrey and Susan Bland, 1991): treats the body's major regulatory systems as an interconnected network, looking for upstream root-cause imbalances rather than treating symptoms in isolation, one real clinical attempt at network-level thinking. Draws real criticism for outrunning its evidence base, but that's a critique of particular protocols, not of the underlying network premise, which network physiology demonstrates independently.
  • All of this is real and evidence-grounded. But not all salutogenic medicine is equally salutogenic: the shallow/deep split is a difference in kind, turning on the distinction between a science's ontology (what's real) and its epistemology (how we know it).

Two Layers of Science

  • Most people conflate the two, identifying "science" with its epistemology (the scientific method) alone.
  • Many integrative-medicine practitioners unconsciously inherit the ontology that historically rode along with that method: metaphysical naturalism.
  • Metaphysical naturalism traces to the Scientific Revolution (Copernicus 1543 → Newton 1687), centered on Descartes's res cogitans/res extensa split — the origin of "spectator consciousness."
  • Its linked commitments: physicalism (no vital forces beyond physics/chemistry); no causally efficacious consciousness; no free will or teleology; reductionism (whole = sum of parts); mechanism (organism as machine).
  • Whether nature's mathematical laws are mind-independent or human constructs is a further question naturalism doesn't cleanly settle — a sign the ontology is less monolithic than usually presented.
  • Key point: metaphysical naturalism is not itself a testable hypothesis — it's a metaphysical stance about what physical findings are allowed to mean, adopted historically and then carried forward as if it were an empirical result.

Shallow Salutogenesis

  • Shallow salutogenesis = a great deal of contemporary "whole-person" medicine: functional medicine, systems-biology-informed integrative care, and much of "energy medicine."
  • What makes it shallow isn't lack of sophistication or clinical value — it's that it stays inside metaphysical naturalism's ontology/epistemology even while reaching for an integrative picture.
  • Real credit due: whole-person and functional medicine are genuine advances over strict reductionism; systems biology/network physiology are a logical next step. These are legitimately salutogenic — just ceilinged by naturalism's ontology.
  • The ceiling shows up exactly when practitioners reach for words like energy, soul, spirit — from inside naturalism's frame, the only available category for anything non-material is "energy" (a measurable physics-style quantity).
  • This flattens genuinely higher-order phenomena (life, consciousness, spirit) into that one category — a category error, and the consistent tell of shallow salutogenesis.
  • Example 1 (already covered above): Levin's bioelectricity work shows a morphospace is not an energy, it's organizing information, but popular accounts collapse it into "bioelectric energy," losing what makes the work important.
  • Example 2: the same error recurs with psychological "space" (Jungian complexes, affective/semantic space) — treating "psychic energy" as a literal subtler electromagnetic quantity is the identical mistake, applied to mind instead of tissue.
  • The etheric and astral have their own proper laws, genuinely outside what "energy" means in physics — not subtle/attenuated energy, but beyond energy in kind. Shallow salutogenesis is sensing this correctly but having no vocabulary for it except the one naturalism supplies.

Deep Salutogenesis

  • Steiner is persistently misread — including by his own followers — when "etheric"/"astral" get imported back into a materialist frame instead of the idealist one he intended. "Etheric energy" just relocates the same category error one level deeper.
  • Deep salutogenesis requires two expansions made together: one ontological, one epistemological.

Expanding the Ontology: From Materialism to Idealism

  • The ontological move: metaphysical naturalism → Idealism, specifically a rigorous contemporary formulation like Bernardo Kastrup's Analytic Idealism — not a vague spiritual gesture.
  • On this view, consciousness/mind is the ground of the world; matter is how mental activity appears from outside, not "dead stuff" that generates mind. An organism is like a localized whirlpool in one underlying stream of consciousness.

Expanding the Epistemology: The Phenomenological Method

  • Ontology alone changes nothing clinically without a matching epistemology: the phenomenological method, originated by Goethe and systematized by Steiner into a ladder of cognitive modes. Where measurement grasps mechanism, phenomenology grasps gesture, pattern, and (at its furthest reach) thought itself directly.
  • Imagination (life level): perceiving polarities/patterns/processes-in-time — an organism's characteristic gesture — rather than a parts list; discloses the etheric formative forces themselves, not just a less-efficient route to the same facts.
  • Inspiration (consciousness level): direct perception of psychological patterns/archetypes — territory Jung independently reached via the collective unconscious.
  • Intuition (furthest reach): grasping purpose, meaning, and one's connection to forces beyond the individual self — where Imagination's gestures and Inspiration's patterns resolve into one overarching archetype (Goethe's Urpflanze belongs here, as the unifying blueprint, not a further gesture).
  • These cultivated modes disclose dimensions outside ordinary physical space-time: the etheric (life-activity information-space), the astral (psychic/soul information-space), and the devachanic (pure thought/spirit information-space).

Beyond Energy: How the Subtle Shapes the Physical

  • The crucial move even many anthroposophic practitioners miss: these spaces interact with the physical world not through physical energies but through something like phase alignment/resonance — shaping physical matter/energy as a formative template, not adding energy to it.
  • "Energy"-talk in integrative medicine (tingling, pressure while scanning a field) is a genuine subtle perception, forced into naturalism's only available vocabulary. The pattern is primary; any physical/energetic trace is a secondary shadow it casts.
  • The morphogenetic field has real scientific history (Gurwitsch's 1922 "embryonic field") predating Sheldrake's more speculative 1980s "morphic resonance" reinterpretation — and the original, more defensible claim is that this field is not itself an energy field or electromagnetic, even though it can shape electromagnetic/bioelectric gradients in the body. Steiner's students Adams and Whicher later formalized this as counterspace using projective geometry.
  • Astral fields behave like a phase field, with topological structure/attractor basins — the same underlying intuition Waddington reached decades earlier via the "epigenetic landscape" image, itself a precursor to Levin's morphospaces.

The Fourfold Human Being, and Why Modern Medicine Never Leaves Earth

  • Steiner's fourfold human being rests on directly perceiving four distinct kinds of formative activity, labeled (for convenience) Earth/Water/Air/Fire. Modern biomedicine — and every "shallow" salutogenic therapy built on its ontology — never actually leaves the Earth level, even when its subject is life, feeling, or spirit.

Aristotle's Four Causes, Recovered

  • Aristotle: a complete "why" needs four causes — material (what it's made of), formal (its organizing pattern), efficient (the source of change), and final (its purpose).
  • Modern biology/medicine narrows this to material cause alone, treating the other three as eliminable or as loose figures of speech — the same Earth-only reduction restated in Aristotle's own terms.
  • Mapped onto Steiner's bodies: material cause↔physical (the mineral substance biomedicine already studies); efficient cause↔etheric (the ongoing formative "software" driving growth/regeneration/repair); formal cause↔astral (Aristotle's own view in De Anima that soul is a living body's formal cause — the ensouled "kind" an organism is); final cause↔devachanic (purpose/meaning as such — Intuition's register).
  • Modern medicine's blindness to three-quarters of Aristotle's own scheme isn't progress superseding an ancient error — it's a modern narrowing mistaken for progress.

Conclusion: Expanding Both, Not Either

  • Deep salutogenesis ≠ salutogenic medicine plus spiritual language. It requires expanding ontology (naturalism → Idealism, so life/soul/spirit are real domains with their own laws) and expanding epistemology in step (measurement alone → the full phenomenological path: Imagination, Inspiration, Intuition).
  • Ontology without matching epistemology is just an unverifiable metaphysical claim. Epistemology without matching ontology is exactly what produces shallow salutogenesis — real perception, forced into a vocabulary ("energy") never built to hold it.
  • Only both together yield a medicine able to treat the whole human being on each level's own proper terms, instead of translating three-quarters of the patient down into the vocabulary of the fourth.
